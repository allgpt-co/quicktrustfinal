# NIST CSF 2.0: Implementation and Review Planning

Use a CSF implementation plan to turn desired cybersecurity outcomes into owned, evidence-backed work. For the framework's concepts and boundaries, start with [NIST Cybersecurity Framework: Functions and Profiles](/blog/pillar-nist-cybersecurity-framework-guide). This companion guide focuses on the working process.

[NIST's CSF 2.0 quick-start guides](https://www.nist.gov/cyberframework/quick-start-guides) explain organizational profiles, Tiers and supporting tools. The sequence below is an editorial planning approach, not a mandatory certification procedure. NIST does not issue a CSF certificate.

## 1. Agree the scope and the reason for the work

List the services, business units, legal entities and systems included in the exercise. Record the decisions the profile is intended to support: internal risk management, a customer discussion, an investment request or a separately identified regulatory obligation. Identify who can approve changes to that scope.

A useful scope statement names the boundaries and dependencies a reviewer might otherwise assume. For example, a cloud service can depend on an outsourced identity provider while its internal finance systems remain outside the selected assessment scope. Write down that distinction instead of allowing it to remain implicit.

## 2. Build a Current Profile from evidence

Review the relevant outcomes with the people responsible for the systems and processes. Separate achieved outcomes, partial implementation and unknowns. Record the evidence location, evidence date, accountable owner and any limitation of the assessment.

Do not convert a policy statement into an implementation conclusion without checking what happens in practice. If the evidence is unavailable, retain that uncertainty in the profile and assign a follow-up action.

## 3. Define a Target Profile and prioritize the differences

Describe the outcomes the organization needs in its circumstances. Consider stakeholder expectations, service dependencies, risk decisions and separately established obligations. Compare the Current and Target Profiles to identify work that needs to be planned.

There is no universal percentage overlap with SOC 2 or ISO 27001, and no fixed CSF Tier proves readiness for those assessments. If another framework is relevant, map its actual criteria and the evidence needed for the chosen scope.

## 4. Turn each priority into a reviewable action

Use a working register with an owner, expected outcome, evidence requirement, target date and dependency. The following structure is an example, not a framework-mandated document:

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Outcome | The CSF outcome and the business reason it matters |
| Present evidence | What is known, when it was checked and what remains uncertain |
| Next action | A concrete change that an accountable owner can deliver |
| Acceptance | How a reviewer will determine whether the intended outcome is achieved |
| Follow-up | The next review date or event that should trigger reassessment |

Keep risk acceptance distinct from completed remediation. Both may be legitimate decisions, but they answer different questions and should have an identifiable decision maker.

## 5. Review the profile when circumstances change

Revisit the profile after material service changes, significant incidents, new customer commitments or changes in the organization's risk assumptions. Preserve earlier versions so reviewers can see what changed and why. An annual calendar entry alone will not capture every material change.

[NIST's FAQs](https://www.nist.gov/cyberframework/faqs) and [official framework resources](https://www.nist.gov/cyberframework) are the primary references for interpreting CSF. Organization-specific legal applicability and independent assurance decisions require their own assessment.
